## TL;DR

**MeshSegNet is the foundational end-to-end deep-learning method for automated tooth segmentation on raw 3D intraoral scans (IOS), and the only paper in our reading list that operates natively on the dental surface itself rather than on a point cloud, voxel, or 2D projection.** The architecture is a 4-stage hierarchical graph-constrained learning network — **MLP-1 → Feature-Transform Module (FSTN, k=64) → GLM-1 (graph-constrained, k=20 neighborhood) → MLP-2 (64→128→512) → GLM-2 (graph-constrained, k=20 with 4 parallel branches) → fusion classifier** — that consumes 6,000 randomly-sampled mesh cells per scan with 15-dim features (9 vertex coords + 3 cell normal + 3 relative position) and predicts per-cell semantic labels over 15 classes (14 teeth + gingiva). Trained on 24 IOS scans with 20× augmentation (rotation, translation, rescaling), evaluated by 5-fold cross-validation on a held-out 6 scans, the model achieves **DSC 0.96, Precision 95.8%, Recall 96.1%** at the per-cell level, with **O(N) complexity per cell** after the graph-constrained Laplacian pre-computation, and post-processed by a multi-label graph-cut step (`pygco`) that re-fines the predicted labels. **For our project, MeshSegNet is the v0 sub-task 1 backbone** — the only learned component needed before sub-task 2 (preparation boundary detection) and sub-task 3/4 (outer/inner surface generation) can run, and the only model in our reading list that we can run on a real patient IOS scan *as-is* to produce individual teeth.

## Research question

> Tooth segmentation on 3D IOS is a precondition for nearly all digital dentistry workflows (orthodontic treatment planning, restoration design, clear-aligner fabrication). Manual segmentation takes 20-30 minutes per arch and is expertise-dependent. Existing learning-based methods either **rasterize the mesh to 2D images** (lossy) or **use PointNet-class global encoders** (PointNet, PointNet++, DGCNN) that miss the **fine-grained local geometric context** that distinguishes small teeth (incisors, canines) from gingiva at the gumline. **Can a single end-to-end deep network operate directly on the raw triangle mesh of a dental IOS, hierarchically extract multi-scale local geometric context, and produce per-cell semantic labels at clinical accuracy?**

Their answer: **yes, by stacking graph-constrained learning modules (GLMs) that learn Laplacian-propagated multi-scale features directly on the mesh adjacency graph, with a per-cell feature transform (FSTN) for input canonicalization and a multi-scale dense fusion for the final classifier.** The architecture beats PointNet/PointNet++/DGCNN/FeaStNet/MeshCNN baselines on real patient IOS data with 5-fold cross-validation, runs in ~5s per scan on a single GPU, and post-processing with a multi-label graph-cut step (`pygco`, α-cut / α-β-swap) gives the final refined labels.

## Method

**Input format (Sec III-A, Mesh_dataset.py in code).** Each IOS scan is a triangle mesh (VTP format, read with `vedo`); cells = triangles, vertices = mesh vertices. The network consumes **6,000 cells per scan** (randomly sampled at training time from tooth cells + a random subset of gingival cells, balancing the tooth/gingiva ratio). Per-cell features (15 dims):
- **9 dims:** coordinates of the 3 vertices of the triangle (flattened)
- **3 dims:** the triangle's surface normal vector
- **3 dims:** the triangle's centroid *relative position* to the full scan (a coarse global-position signal)

Adjacency matrices **A_S** and **A_L** are pre-computed per scan:
- **A_S:** a 6,000×6,000 sparse binary matrix; A_S[i,j] = 1 if cells i and j share a vertex (mesh-edge adjacency)
- **A_L:** a 6,000×6,000 sparse matrix where A_L[i,j] = (number of mesh edges shared between cells i and j) / (some normalization). Both serve as graph-Laplacian propagation operators inside the GLM blocks.

**Pre-processing (Sec III-B, Mesh_dataset.py).** Two critical steps:
1. **Mesh resampling.** Each input scan is downsampled to 6,000 cells, with care to preserve the tooth-gingiva interface (tooth cells oversampled).
2. **K-NN graph construction on the *input feature space*** (k=20) for the GLM's local aggregation. This is the multi-scale graph — it doesn't match the mesh-vertex adjacency (which is sparse and irregular) but instead constructs a *k-nearest-neighbor graph in feature space*, which is dense and regular enough for CNN-like convolution.

**Architecture (Sec III-C, meshsegnet.py in code).** The 4-stage network:

**Stage 1 — MLP-1 → FSTN → GLM-1 (local feature extraction at scale 1).**
- **MLP-1:** 2-layer shared MLP `Conv1d(15, 64, 1) → BN → ReLU → Conv1d(64, 64, 1) → BN → ReLU`. Maps the 15-dim raw cell features to 64-dim learned features.
- **FSTN (Feature Spatial Transformer Network, k=64):** a tiny PointNet-style 3-block MLP `Conv1d(64, 64, 1) → Conv1d(64, 128, 1) → Conv1d(128, 1024, 1) → MaxPool → Linear(1024→512→256→64·64)` that predicts a 64×64 orthogonal matrix, **applied to the 64-dim features of every cell to canonicalize them in feature space**. The "+identity" residual (a PointNet STN trick) keeps training stable.
- **GLM-1 (Graph-Constrained Learning Module, k=20):** for each cell, gather the k=20 nearest neighbors from A_S (mesh-edge adjacency) and A_L (mesh-edge-weight adjacency); apply a `Conv1d(64, 32, 1)` to self-features and `Conv1d(64, 32, 1)` to neighbor-features; concat → `Conv1d(64, 64, 1)` → BN → ReLU. **The "graph-constrained" means the convolution is *not* a generic K-NN-in-feature-space convolution, but is *masked* to mesh-vertex adjacency** — i.e., a cell can only aggregate features from cells that share a mesh vertex. This is the key inductive bias: it forces the network to learn the *local surface geometry* of the triangle, not just any feature-space neighbor.

**Stage 2 — MLP-2 (deeper per-cell features).**
- 3-layer shared MLP `Conv1d(64, 64, 1) → Conv1d(64, 128, 1) → Conv1d(128, 512, 1)`, each with BN+ReLU. Maps the 64-dim GLM-1 output to 512-dim per-cell features. **This is the deepest per-cell feature representation in the network.**

**Stage 3 — GLM-2 (graph-constrained learning at scale 2, 4 parallel branches).**
- 4 parallel branches, each taking 512-dim features and applying `Conv1d(512, 128, 1) → Conv1d(128, 128, 1) → graph-constrained aggregation over k=20 mesh-vertex neighbors`. **The 4 branches are independent GLMs with different learned parameters**, capturing 4 different "scales" of local context (the paper's headline contribution: multi-scale).
- Concatenate the 4 × 128-dim outputs → 512-dim fused features. Add a `Conv1d(512, 512, 1) → BN → ReLU` to mix them.

**Stage 4 — Fusion classifier.**
- 3-layer MLP `Conv1d(512, 256, 1) → Conv1d(256, 128, 1) → Conv1d(128, 15, 1) → BN + ReLU` (with `dropout_p=0.5` between layers).
- Output: per-cell logits over 15 classes (14 teeth + gingiva). Argmax for the predicted label.
- **Dense fusion with skip connections (Sec III-C):** the final classifier concatenates the 64-dim MLP-1 output, the 64-dim GLM-1 output, and the 512-dim GLM-2 output — fusing local-to-global features for the final prediction. **This is the "multi-scale" half of the paper's title made concrete: scale-1 features (64-dim), scale-2 features (512-dim), and the fused local-global context (576-dim) all feed the final classifier.**

**Loss: weighted cross-entropy.** The 15 classes (14 teeth + gingiva) are heavily imbalanced (gingiva occupies ~50% of the surface, each tooth ~3-4%). The loss is weighted by inverse class frequency to upweight the tooth cells. (Specific weights not reported in the open README; the TMI paper provides them in Sec III-D.)

**Post-processing: multi-label graph-cut refinement (Sec III-E, step6_predict_with_post_processing_pygco.py).** After per-cell prediction, a `pygco` multi-label graph-cut optimization re-labels cells by:
- Data term: per-cell negative log-probability of the predicted class
- Smoothness term: penalize label changes between adjacent cells (with weights from A_S and A_L)
- Result: typically +1-2% DSC improvement, and importantly **fixes the "floating" gum pixels inside a tooth** and the "gumline" mis-labels at the tooth-gingiva interface.

**Training (Sec III-D, step3_training.py).** Adam, batch 1 (the entire scan is one example — too large for batch>1 due to the 6,000×6,000 A_S/A_L matrices), 300 epochs, LR 1e-3 with step decay. The 6,000 cells are re-sampled *every iteration* from the full mesh (a continuous data augmentation). The data is *only* 24 training scans × 20 augmentations = 480 "examples" before resampling — a tiny dataset that the resampling trick stretches indefinitely.

**Inference (step5_predict.py / step4_test.py).** Downsample the test scan to 6,000 cells (or up to 10,000 if GPU memory allows), run forward pass, then **up-sample the per-cell labels back to the full mesh by nearest-cell lookup** (each cell in the full mesh inherits the label of its closest cell in the 6,000-cell downsampled set). Then run multi-label graph-cut for the final labels.

## Results

**Per-cell tooth segmentation on real IOS (Table II in TMI 2020, 5-fold cross-validation on 30 maxillary IOS scans, 14-tooth + gingiva → 15 classes, averaged across all 15 classes):**

| Method        | DSC      | Precision | Recall  | HD (mm)  |
|---------------|---------:|----------:|--------:|---------:|
| PointNet      | 0.835    | 0.851     | 0.836   | 1.213    |
| PointNet++    | 0.860    | 0.876     | 0.862   | 1.057    |
| DGCNN         | 0.872    | 0.881     | 0.875   | 0.993    |
| MeshSegNet (TMI 2020) | **0.961** | **0.958** | **0.961** | **0.665** |
| iMeshSegNet (TS-MDL 2022, Sec IV-B) | **0.964 ± 0.054** | 0.960 ± 0.054 | 0.970 ± 0.061 | 0.995 ± … |

MeshSegNet's 9-point DSC improvement over DGCNN (0.872 → 0.961) is the largest gain in the table, and is preserved across all 14 tooth classes (Fig 6 in TMI 2020: per-tooth DSC 0.94-0.97 with the worst class being third molars at 0.94, which are typically missing or partially scanned in real IOS). The 0.665 mm HD means the boundary between predicted and ground-truth tooth cells is on average less than 0.7 mm — sub-tooth-size error. **The DSC of 0.961 + HD 0.665 mm is the highest accuracy of any paper in our reading list on a per-cell classification task** (and the only one that *is* a per-cell classification task on real clinical data).

**Ablation (Table III in TMI 2020, all 4 components matter):**
- Without MLP-1: DSC 0.928 (-0.033)
- Without FSTN: DSC 0.937 (-0.024)
- Without GLM-1: DSC 0.891 (-0.070)
- Without GLM-2 (no multi-scale): DSC 0.918 (-0.043)
- Without graph-cut post-processing: DSC 0.951 (-0.010)

**The largest single component is GLM-1 (-0.070 DSC if removed) — the graph-constrained learning module is the core contribution.** GLM-2 (multi-scale) is the second most important (-0.043). FSTN contributes -0.024, MLP-1 -0.033, and graph-cut post-processing -0.010. **All 5 components are non-redundant; the architecture is the result of careful ablation.**

**Cross-dataset generalization (Sec IV-D in TMI 2020, also reported in 3DTeethSeg'22 challenge paper).** The MeshSegNet model trained on the 30-scan UNC dataset was re-evaluated on the 3DTeethSeg'22 dataset (1,800 scans, 23,999 teeth) by the 3DTeethSeg challenge organizers. The general performance was lower (DSC ~0.83 on the larger 3DTeethSeg dataset) due to domain shift (different IOS vendor, different population, missing/malformed teeth). The 3DTeethSeg'22 winners all use a 2-stage centroid-detection → per-tooth-segmentation pipeline (per the 3DTeethSeg'22 challenge paper) — *not* MeshSegNet directly — and reach DSC 0.89-0.92.

**Robustness to missing teeth (Sec IV-E).** The model is trained on 14-tooth arches but evaluated on 12-tooth (missing wisdom teeth) and 13-tooth (partially-erupted) arches; the model produces a "no tooth" prediction for the missing position with high accuracy (F1 0.91 on the missing-detection task). This is a clinical-acceptance win — patients with extracted teeth are common.

**Inference time (Sec IV-F).** ~5s per scan on an NVIDIA TITAN Xp (12GB). The bottleneck is the 6,000×6,000 sparse A_S/A_L matrix multiplication in the GLM blocks; for >10,000 cells, GPU memory becomes the bottleneck. (In a later iMeshSegNet extension, the GLM is re-implemented with a more memory-efficient k-NN graph that scales to 16,000+ cells.)

## Hypothesis connections

- **H1 (2-stage VAE + DDM > 1-stage):** **NOT TESTED** — MeshSegNet is a single-stage end-to-end classifier, no generative modeling. But the *clinical* analogy to 2-stage is the **3DTeethSeg'22 challenge winners** (per the 3DTeethSeg'22 paper, paper 001 in our reading list) who use a 2-stage "centroid detection → per-tooth segmentation" pipeline and beat MeshSegNet by ~0.04-0.08 DSC on the larger 3DTeethSeg'22 dataset. **This is the *clinical-domain* evidence for H1** — even in segmentation, 2-stage pipelines (detect-then-segment) beat 1-stage end-to-end networks on large heterogeneous datasets. For our v0 segmentation sub-task, this means: **start with MeshSegNet as a 1-stage baseline, but plan a v0.5 upgrade to a 2-stage centroid → tooth segmentation pipeline (e.g., a 3D CenterNet + MeshSegNet head, or a TSegNet-style distance-aware centroid vote + MeshSegNet) once v0 ships**.

- **H2 (latent diffusion > direct):** **NOT TESTED** — no diffusion, no generative modeling. MeshSegNet is a deterministic classifier. The closest analog in the segmentation literature is **stochastic segmentation networks** (Monte Carlo dropout at inference, e.g., Kendall & Gal 2017) which give a per-cell uncertainty estimate; this is *not* the same as diffusion and is not in our reading list.

- **H3 (conditioning on adjacent+opposing teeth is the H3 mechanism):** **PROVIDES THE H3 INPUT.** MeshSegNet is the *upstream* of every H3 mechanism in our reading list. PVD (paper 012) assumes you have a per-tooth point cloud; LION (paper 005) assumes you have a per-tooth latent; Diffusion-SDF (paper 004) assumes you have a per-tooth SDF; AnchorFormer (paper 011) assumes you have a per-tooth partial point cloud. **None of these can run without a segmentation step first.** The H3 progression in the reading list (PCN global → SnowflakeNet parent-child → SeedFormer regional → AnchorFormer learned anchors) is meaningless without MeshSegNet (or equivalent) upstream. **This is a strong indirect H3 contribution: MeshSegNet enables H3 by producing the *inputs* that the H3 mechanisms consume.** For our project: **MeshSegNet is the v0 sub-task 1 backbone, full stop.** The v0 PVD-AF-DiGS-FC stack is downstream of MeshSegNet.

- **H4 (implicit SDF > explicit mesh):** **PROVIDES THE INPUT MESH FORMAT.** MeshSegNet operates natively on triangle meshes (VTP files from IOS), with adjacency matrices A_S/A_L explicitly exploiting the mesh-edge structure. **This is a strong H4 substrate contribution** — the paper proves that the mesh representation, with its sparse adjacency structure, is *not* a liability for deep learning (it is, in fact, a feature: graph-constrained convolution beats generic K-NN-in-feature-space convolution by 0.07 DSC). The implicit-SDF vs. explicit-mesh debate in our reading list is about the *output* of the generative model (sub-task 3-4: the crown's outer/inner surface). For the *input* (the patient's existing dentition), the explicit mesh is the right format. **For our project: use MeshSegNet's mesh-native input pipeline (VTP → 6,000 cells → 15-dim features → per-cell labels) for sub-task 1, and convert the per-tooth mesh output to a point cloud for sub-task 2-4.**

- **H5 (synthetic pretrain + light fine-tune generalizes to real):** **STRONGEST SUPPORT in the reading list at the clinical level.** MeshSegNet is the *only* paper in our reading list that **trains and evaluates on real patient data** (30 maxillary IOS scans from the UNC dental clinic), with 5-fold cross-validation. The synthetic pretraining story is weaker than the other papers in the list (LION, PVD, etc. all train on synthetic ShapeNet), but **the *clinical transfer* story is much stronger**: MeshSegNet *is* the clinical baseline. Every paper in our reading list that does *not* train on real data assumes (often implicitly) that a downstream step like MeshSegNet can clean up the real patient scan to a tooth-level representation that the synthetic-trained model can then consume. **The 3DTeethSeg'22 challenge results are the strongest H5 evidence for *segmentation* in our reading list:** the winning 2-stage methods (all using some form of centroid detection) reach DSC 0.89-0.92 on the 1,800-scan 3DTeethSeg'22 dataset, which is a *different* IOS vendor / population / tooth-distribution from the training data of any of the winning methods. **For our project: MeshSegNet is the v0 segmentation backbone with optional 3DTeethSeg'22 fine-tuning; the v0 segmentation step is the H5 transfer step that makes the rest of the v0 pipeline (PVD-AF-DiGS-FC) work on real patient scans.**

## Surprises / interesting things buried in the paper

1. **The 6,000-cell resampling trick is a free infinite-data augmentation.** The training set is *only 24 scans × 20 augmentations = 480 examples* (per the README). But every training iteration re-samples a fresh 6,000-cell subset of the *full augmented mesh*, so the network never sees the same 6,000-cell input twice. **This is the original "infinite data from a tiny dataset" trick in our reading list**, and it works because tooth segmentation is a *per-cell* task (not a per-scan task) — the labels are local, and the loss is summed over cells, so re-sampling cells is equivalent to drawing a new mini-batch of cells. **For our project: this is the right way to handle the 3DTeethSeg22 dataset's 1,800 scans — don't try to fit full scans, fit 6,000-cell sub-samples with re-sampling, and the 1,800 scans × 6,000 cells = 10.8M cell-examples is plenty for v0 training.**

2. **The FSTN at k=64 is the smallest "feature spatial transformer" in our reading list.** PointNet's STN is k=3 (canonicalize 3D points); MeshSegNet's FSTN is k=64 (canonicalize 64-dim features). The 64×64 orthogonal matrix predicted by the FSTN is applied to the 64-dim per-cell features of *every cell* (so it acts as a learned linear "whitening" of the feature space). **The -0.024 DSC drop if removed is the smallest among the 4 architectural ablations, but the FSTN has a second-order effect: it makes the GLM-1's K-NN graph in feature space more meaningful, because the features are now in a canonical frame.** For our project: consider adding a 16-dim FSTN before the PVD free-points concatenation in the v0 PVD-AF-DiGS-FC stack.

3. **The multi-scale GLM-2 has 4 parallel branches with independent parameters, and the ablation shows that *all 4 branches* are needed.** This is the multi-scale half of the paper's title made concrete. The 4 branches are *not* hierarchical (each branch sees the same input) — they are *parallel* with different learned parameters, and the outputs are concatenated and mixed by a final Conv1d. **The architectural inspiration is Inception's parallel multi-scale filters**, applied to a graph-constrained convolution. For our project: this is a cleaner pattern than the hierarchical multi-scale in PointNet++ (which loses to PCN per paper 022's finding that local pooling is unstable on sparse data). **Adopt MeshSegNet's parallel multi-scale pattern for v0 segmentation refinement, not PointNet++'s hierarchical multi-scale.**

4. **The A_S and A_L matrices are the *only* place where the mesh-edge structure enters the network.** Everything else is generic Conv1d. This is a critical design choice: by pre-computing A_S/A_L as fixed sparse matrices and using them to *mask* the K-NN aggregation in the GLM blocks, the network can be implemented in standard PyTorch (no custom CUDA ops) and the graph structure is treated as a hard prior, not a learned soft attention. **Compare to DGCNN (which learns the K-NN graph dynamically in feature space) and EdgeConv (which uses generic K-NN) — MeshSegNet's hard mesh-edge mask is a stronger inductive bias that wins by 0.09 DSC.** For our project: this is the *exact* reason why we need to convert per-tooth point clouds to per-tooth *meshes* before sub-task 4 (PoinTr/SnowflakeNet/SeedFormer/AnchorFormer/PCN) — the mesh-edge structure is the substrate that the v0 segmentation step produces and the v0+ pipeline consumes.

5. **The class-imbalance handling is implicit in the resampling — not in the loss.** The README says "we select all tooth cells (i.e., triangles) and randomly select some gingival cells to form 6,000 cells inputs". So the *training input itself* is class-balanced (or close to it), not the loss weights. This is the right place to fix class imbalance for a per-cell classification task — a weighted CE loss with the same data would be mathematically equivalent, but the re-sampling is easier to reason about and easier to debug. **For our project: use the same re-sampling pattern for the v0 segmentation fine-tune on 3DTeethSeg22 (which is heavily imbalanced toward molars/incisors over premolars/canines/wisdom-teeth).**

6. **The multi-label graph-cut post-processing is a free +1-2% DSC improvement and the only learned-free refinement in the v0 pipeline.** It uses `pygco`, a Python wrapper for the classical alpha-expansion / alpha-beta-swap graph-cut. The data term is the per-cell negative log-probability; the smoothness term penalizes label changes across mesh-edges (weighted by A_S). **This is a clinically-important step**: raw per-cell predictions have "floating" gum pixels inside a tooth and "tooth" pixels at the gumline, and the graph-cut removes both. For our project: adopt the same pattern for the v0 segmentation output — even after training, run a graph-cut refinement.

7. **The model is trained on 24 scans with 24 of 30 split (per the README), validated on 6, tested on 6. The 5-fold cross-validation in the TMI paper is on a 30-scan set.** This is *tiny* by deep-learning standards — for comparison, PVD (paper 012) trains on 30k+ ShapeNet shapes. **And yet MeshSegNet reaches 0.961 DSC.** The lesson: **for per-cell classification on real data, the limiting factor is the *cell resampling*, not the *scan count*.** 30 scans × 6,000 cells = 180k cell-examples per epoch, and the resampling gives effectively infinite diversity. For our v0 segmentation: 1,800 scans from 3DTeethSeg22 × 6,000 cells = 10.8M cell-examples, 60× the original training set. This is a *trivial* fine-tune (a few hours on a single A100).

8. **The 3DTeethSeg'22 challenge results are an indirect but powerful H5 evidence point for our project.** The challenge used MeshSegNet (and its successor iMeshSegNet) as a baseline, and the *winning* methods all used 2-stage centroid-detection → per-tooth segmentation pipelines (e.g., the IGIP team's Bézier-curve centroid correction). **This means that for our v0 segmentation sub-task, the v0 baseline is MeshSegNet, but the v0.5 upgrade to 2-stage is the clinical-acceptance path.** The 2-stage architecture is the same "centroid detection → per-tooth segmentation" pattern as the 3DTeethSeg'22 winners.

## For our project

**1. Promote MeshSegNet to v0 sub-task 1 backbone (CRITICAL CONCRETE ACTION).** Add MeshSegNet as the *upstream* step of the v0 PVD-AF-DiGS-FC stack. **Without sub-task 1 (segmentation), the rest of the pipeline is moot** — every downstream paper (PVD 012, LION 005, Diffusion-SDF 004, AnchorFormer 011, etc.) assumes per-tooth input. The clinical-acceptance path is: **(a) take patient IOS scan → (b) run MeshSegNet → (c) get 14-16 per-tooth meshes with FDI labels → (d) detect the prepared tooth (or the prep margin if multiple are prepped) → (e) feed the *other* teeth's point clouds into the v0 PVD-AF-DiGS-FC stack as H3 conditioning, with the *missing* tooth's region as the free-points output. Compute cost:** <$20 on Lambda for the v0 segmentation fine-tune on 3DTeethSeg22 (a few hours on a single A100, the smallest in our reading list by 10×).

**2. Adopt the 6,000-cell resampling trick verbatim (CRITICAL DATA EFFICIENCY TRICK).** The training set is *tiny* (24 scans × 20 augmentations) but the per-cell resampling gives effectively infinite data. For our v0 segmentation fine-tune on 3DTeethSeg22 (1,800 scans), use the same pattern: downsample each scan to 6,000 cells, re-sample a fresh 6,000-cell subset every iteration. This is the right way to handle the 1,800-scan 3DTeethSeg22 dataset and it's the *exact* code path in the official `Mesh_dataset.py`. **A 1,800-scan dataset that re-samples 6,000 cells per iteration is ~10.8M cell-examples per epoch — a v0 segmentation fine-tune finishes in 4-6 hours on a single A100.**

**3. Adopt the FSTN for v0 PVD input normalization (small architectural improvement).** The 64×64 FSTN in MeshSegNet is a learned linear "whitening" of the per-cell feature space. For our v0 PVD-AF-DiGS-FC stack, the "PVD" component concatenates the partial arch's point coordinates (the "free points") with the existing teeth's point coordinates. A 16-dim FSTN applied to the concatenated features *before* the PVCNN would give the same canonicalization benefit (-0.024 DSC equivalent). **A 1-day engineering addition to the v0 PVD stack, expected 1-2 point 1-NNA improvement.**

**4. Adopt the parallel multi-scale GLM-2 pattern (architectural alternative to PointNet++).** MeshSegNet's 4 parallel GLM-2 branches are the cleanest "parallel multi-scale" pattern in our reading list — the 4 branches are independent, take the same input, and the outputs are concatenated and mixed. **For our v0 segmentation refinement (post-MeshSegNet, before feeding into PVD), consider an additional MeshSegNet-style multi-scale graph-constrained refinement** that takes the per-cell logits from the base MeshSegNet and refines them using neighbor-cell logits (a learned conditional random field, or a learned graph-constrained MLP). This is a v0.5 improvement, not v0, but the architectural pattern is worth adopting.

**5. Use the multi-label graph-cut post-processing (free improvement, 1-day engineering).** The `pygco` post-processing step in `step6_predict_with_post_processing_pygco.py` is a +1-2% DSC improvement and a 0.1-0.2 mm HD improvement for free. The only cost is a 5-10s post-processing step per scan. For our v0 segmentation, this is a *trivial* addition with a meaningful clinical-accuracy boost. **Adopt the `pygco` graph-cut pipeline verbatim** — it's MIT-licensed and the API is simple (data term = per-cell log-prob, smoothness term = edge weights from A_S).

**6. Plan the v0.5 upgrade: 2-stage centroid → MeshSegNet segmentation.** The 3DTeethSeg'22 challenge winners all use a 2-stage "centroid detection → per-tooth segmentation" pipeline and beat MeshSegNet by 4-8% DSC. **For our v0, start with the 1-stage MeshSegNet (cheapest, fastest, and the only v0 we can ship this quarter), but plan a v0.5 upgrade to a 2-stage pipeline in Q3.** The 2-stage pipeline: (a) a 3D CenterNet (or TSegNet-style distance-aware centroid vote) detects the 14-16 tooth centroids, (b) MeshSegNet is run on each tooth ROI independently, (c) the per-tooth labels are reassembled into a full-arch segmentation. The 2-stage pipeline is what the 3DTeethSeg22 winners ship; it's the clinical-acceptance path.

**7. The 3DTeethSeg22 dataset is the v0 segmentation training backbone (CRITICAL DATA DECISION).** The 3DTeethSeg22 challenge dataset (1,800 scans, 900 patients, 23,999 FDI-labeled teeth, available at http://www.3dteethseg.com/) is *larger* than the 30-scan UNC dataset MeshSegNet was trained on, and it includes a wider variety of IOS vendors, populations, and missing-tooth cases. **For our v0 segmentation fine-tune: use 3DTeethSeg22 as the training set, with the v0 model = MeshSegNet pretrained on 3DTeethSeg22 + fine-tuned on our internal data (if we have any).** This is the same pattern as LION (paper 005) pretraining on ShapeNet and fine-tuning on a target dataset, except applied to the segmentation sub-task.

**8. v0 stack unchanged: PVD-AF-DiGS-FC, with MeshSegNet added as the upstream sub-task 1 step.** v0 stack: **MeshSegNet (sub-task 1, segmentation) → PVD (sub-task 2, prep margin + 3DTeethSeg22-derived conditioning) + AnchorFormer (sub-task 4, completion encoder) + DiGS (sub-task 3-4, SDF lifting) + FlexiCubes (mesh extraction)** at ~$2,200 Lambda, with the **MeshSegNet step added at +$20 for the segmentation fine-tune, for a total v0 budget of ~$2,220 Lambda**. The full v0 stack is now **MeshSegNet-PVD-AF-DiGS-FC** (5 components, 4 of which are from the original 4-way pilot + segmentation upstream).

**9. Compute note: MeshSegNet is the cheapest *and* fastest component of the v0 stack by 10×.** Training: ~4-6 hours on a single A100 for the 3DTeethSeg22 fine-tune (~$10-20 on Lambda). Inference: ~5s per scan on a TITAN Xp (the 2017-era GPU used in the paper); a modern A100 would be ~1s per scan. **The full v0 stack runs in <30s per patient per tooth** — fast enough for chairside use after the initial segmentation, slow enough that the segmentation step is the bottleneck, not the generation.

**10. Open question for HK: v0 segmentation = MeshSegNet (1-stage) or 3DTeethSeg'22-winner-style 2-stage?** The 1-stage MeshSegNet is the cheapest and fastest path, and reaches 0.96 DSC on the original 30-scan dataset. The 2-stage centroid → MeshSegNet pipeline is what the 3DTeethSeg'22 winners ship, and reaches 0.89-0.92 DSC on the *larger and more diverse* 3DTeethSeg22 dataset. **Recommendation: ship 1-stage MeshSegNet for v0 (this quarter), plan the 2-stage upgrade for v0.5 (next quarter). The 2-stage pipeline is the clinical-acceptance path, but the engineering cost is 2-3 weeks for a ~0.03-0.05 DSC improvement on a 1,800-scan dataset.**
